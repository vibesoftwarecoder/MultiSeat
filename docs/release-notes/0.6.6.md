**A seat no longer reports Ready until its Apollo can actually answer. Before, a seat counted as
ready as soon as Apollo had a process ID, and an Apollo that failed a few seconds later looked
healthy until something else noticed.**

## A seat said it was ready before Apollo was

Starting a seat's Apollo used to end the moment the process launched. Apollo still has work to do
after that — it opens its web server, sets up TLS and prepares its encoder — and any of those can
fail. In #54, Apollo exited about 13 seconds after launch, over and over, while the seat had already
been reported ready.

MultiSeat now waits for Apollo's own API. After launch it asks the seat's `serverinfo` endpoint for
up to 30 seconds. Three things must all be true before the seat counts as ready:

- the reply is a successful `serverinfo` answer, not just an open port or some other HTTP 200
- the reply carries this seat's own identity, so another seat's Apollo cannot stand in for it
- the Apollo process that was launched is still running when the reply arrives

If Apollo exits first, or the 30 seconds run out, MultiSeat stops the process it launched and the
error names the seat's Apollo log. Automatic restarts go through the same check and keep their limit
of three attempts. While a restart is waiting, the seat shows `Connecting`.

## The warning no longer blames the encoder

When Apollo exited soon after starting, the service used to say an encoder had failed to open. The
timing does not prove that. Apollo also waits ten seconds before exiting when its web server or TLS
setup fails, which fits the 13 seconds in #54 just as well. The warning now says the cause is not
known from timing alone and points at the Apollo log. It still explains how to turn on `verbose`
logging when the log says nothing, and that `debug` is not enough.

## Other changes you may notice

- After the host wakes from sleep, a seat comes back as `Ready` rather than `Streaming`. A working
  API does not prove the client resumed its stream.
- After a wake, an Apollo that never becomes ready now puts the seat in `Error`, once its restart
  attempts are used up. Before, the seat kept its old status and a later health check tried again.
- A failing restart can now take up to about 90 seconds (three attempts of up to 30 seconds each)
  before the seat reports `Error`.
- The log message that says a seat's Apollo is ready now gives the Moonlight port. It used to give
  the port one higher, which is the web UI's.

## How this was checked

The new behaviour is covered by 14 new tests, and all 560 tests pass in CI. They check a refused
connection, another seat's reply, an HTTP 200 with the wrong content, an HTTP error, a process that
exits before or during the request, a request that hangs past the deadline, and cancellation. One
test starts a real local `serverinfo` server and confirms the seat becomes ready without its process
being stopped.

⚠️ **Not yet verified on real hardware.** Unlike 0.6.5, no seat was provisioned with this build
before release.

## Install

```powershell
.\prerequisites\install-prerequisites.ps1     # drivers - needed either way
.\scripts\install-service.ps1 -FromZip .
```

Your configuration is preserved, as of 0.6.4 — `appsettings.json` and `appsettings.local.json` are
kept byte for byte, with a backup under `C:\ProgramData\MultiSeat\config-backups\`.

Afterwards, confirm what landed:

```powershell
& 'C:\Program Files\MultiSeat\MultiSeat.Service.exe' --config
```

It should report `0.6.6`.

## Known limitations

- **Seats capture the remote desktop surface, not a dedicated virtual display.** A seat's display
  reports a 1000 Hz refresh rate, which is the sign of it. A Windows constraint on where a virtual
  display can attach, not a regression.
- **Tearing a seat down still briefly disturbs a standalone Apollo.** Announced rather than silent,
  but unchanged.
- **This release does not fix what makes Apollo exit in #54.** It makes the failure visible sooner
  and describes it honestly. The cause still has to come from Apollo's log.
